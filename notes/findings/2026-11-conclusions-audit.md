# Conclusions audit: the 2022 paper against the evidence

14 November 2026 (section 6 added 23 November, section 7 added 27 November). Decision-point review after the delegated tasks, the browser pass and Strand D (`findings-2026-11-decision-point-2.md`, `findings-2026-11-browser-pass.md`, `findings-2026-11-strand-d-english.md`). It sets each claim of "Who Numbered the Crusades?" (2022 draft) against what has been found, states the thesis the evidence now supports, and lists the gaps that still block an answer to the paper's own questions.

Evidence grades used below: **firm** (read at full text, wording quoted, more than one witness), **provisional** (read from OCR or one witness; page image or title page not yet checked), **open** (not researched).

## 1. The thesis the evidence now supports

The modern eight was assembled in three layers, by three different kinds of book, over 150 years.

1. **The frame (by 1689–1701): eight, Holy Land only, Tunis last.** Moréri's dictionary has eight numbered crusades by 1689. Basnage's Furetière (1701) supplies the formula "On compte 8. Croisades." The list is closed to expeditions "pour la conquête de la Terre Sainte" while the same entries define *croisade* to include wars on heretics and pagans. In this frame Henry VI is fourth and one long sixth covers 1213–44. French and English reference works repeat it to 1838, Britannica to 1854. **Firm.**
2. **The slots (attested 1771): 1202–04 fourth, Andrew of Hungary fifth, Frederick II sixth, Louis IX seventh.** Koch's *Tableau des révolutions de l'Europe* (1771): "On en compte proprement sept." No Henry VI, no numbered Tunis. Repeated by Schöll (1825, 1830), Saigey (Hachette, 1834), Lamp (1839). **Provisional** (archive.org OCR; title page read in OCR only).
3. **The merger (Paris, by 1840–42): Koch's slots inside the dictionaries' eight.** Rendu and Ansart's course for the écoles normales primaires, written to the official programme (2nd ed. 1841–42; 1st ed. 1840–41 unread); Bouillet (1842); Le Bas (1842); the *Complément du Dictionnaire de l'Académie* (1842), which names the Henry VI count as the minority and says "Nous avons suivi l'usage le plus général." Lefranc's school history still has the old eight in 1837. **Provisional**: the *Complément* was read from OCR search hits, and the earliest witness is a second edition.

After the merger the scheme travels by schoolbook and translation, not by scholarship: Duruy (1861, firm); into English through Greene's 1851 translation of Rendu (firm), then Beale (1858), Tainsh, Gazeau, Steele, Myers. English reference works held older imported counts until Bréhier (1908) and Barker in Britannica's 11th edition (1911), whose text was printed to 1962. Wikipedia's first article (2001) carries Bréhier's eight date ranges.

In one sentence, for the article: **the crusades were numbered by dictionary compilers, a Strasbourg professor's textbook and the French school system; crusade historians and English popular writers received the numbers and did not make them.**

## 3. Feedback on the last round

**What went right.** The Koch find, the *Complément*'s statement of custom about the slots, and Greene-to-Rendu are the three most valuable results since the dictionary stemma. Strand D settles claim 10 against the paper.

**What needs attention.**

1. **The earliest-found trap, a third time.** Mombert gave way to Bouillet and Bouillet to Koch. "The slots are Koch's" is the same kind of claim. Koch writes "on en compte *proprement* sept," which reads as a correction of some other count; he may be reporting a Strasbourg or German university usage. Write "attested in Koch (1771)" until the German and Latin compendia of 1700–70 have been checked.
2. **The weakest link has moved; it has not gone.** It is now the first edition of Rendu (1840–41), which is not digitized (BnF G-18709), and the official programme it was written to. If the programme itself lists the crusades, that is the answer to the title. Nothing else in the project is worth as much per hour.
3. **Evidence quality fell while coverage rose.** Several load-bearing rows are OCR search hits (the *Complément*, Ruelle, Abel Hugo), translations standing in for originals (Koch 1823 via Crichton), or quotations that passed through a summarizer in the first agent run. None has had a second reader. About a dozen pages now carry the argument and the author should read each one.
4. **The delegation failed on permissions, not on intelligence.** The first agent run could not open constructed URLs and returned mostly "not reached." The useful results came from the browser sessions afterward. Future briefs need either pre-approved hosts or exact URLs in the brief.
5. **Scope grew again.** Strand D ran to 1967 and included a "last Henry VI fourth" search that no brief asked for. It was productive, but the research log in the project still ends on 10 November, the Next list is stale, four proposed codebook amendments are undecided, and rows now sit in at least six tables with duplicates across frames. Reconcile before the next round or the dataset will not be citable.
6. **Half of the paper's abstract has had no research.** The abstract asks how the system emerged "and why it fell from favor." Every session so far has worked on emergence.

## 4. The gaps, ranked by what they block

**A. Blocking the answer to the title question**

1. *The merger.* Rendu and Ansart, first edition (1840–41); the Conseil royal programme for the écoles normales; Lefranc's 1841 edition; Ruelle and Huillard-Bréholles (1842); Abel Hugo (1839). One question: which French book first puts a numbered eighth at Tunis on top of the Koch slots, and does an official text stand behind it?
2. *The two roots.* Where Moréri's eight comes from (Maimbourg? the 1674–83 editions are unread), and what Koch was correcting (German and Strasbourg compendia, 1700–70).

**B. Blocking the paper's "why" questions**

3. *Reasons, not counts.* The paper asks why Henry VI, the Barons' Crusade and the Crusade of 1101 got no number. The project has collected hundreds of counts and three stated reasons (Lévi Alvarès refusing Frederick II, 1835; Pierer on 1202–04; the *Complément*, 1842). A table of every explicit justification for including or excluding an expedition is the only way to answer this, and nobody has built it.
4. *The fall.* When and why professional historians stopped at five: Stevenson, Grousset, Runciman, Setton, Mayer, Riley-Smith, and current French usage. Claims 21 and 22 depend on it. Unresearched.

**C. Blocking the diffusion claims**

5. *France, 1842–1914.* The panel is open: Duruy is confirmed, Larousse dissents, nothing connects them. No share can be stated.
6. *Germany.* No German reference entry from 1709 to 1911 has been read under the protocol, and the origin of the German seven (a relative of Koch's seven?) is unknown.
7. *Italy and the rest.* Cantù's switch between 1844 and 1851 is still undated.

**D. Smaller**

8. Edward alone as "Ninth Crusade" before 2003.
9. Verification: page images, title pages, a second coder on bracket rows.
10. The International Medieval Bibliography, RI-OPAC and JSTOR, before claiming priority in print.

## 5. Recommendation

Stop widening. One more bounded round on gaps 1, 3 and 4, then draft.

- Gap 1 is partly a human task: order the BnF reproduction of Rendu's first edition, or find the programme text in the *Bulletin universitaire* or *Manuel général de l'instruction primaire*. An Opus session can chase the programme and the three 1839–42 books.
- Gap 3 is a Sonnet task over text already collected, plus the entries already located.
- Gap 4 is an Opus task with a short reading list; most of it is twentieth-century and in copyright, so it runs on contents pages, prefaces and reviews.

The article can keep the 2022 paper's skeleton, which is sound: Constable's call, the early modern prehistory, the national traditions, the table, the question, the afterlife. The answer in the middle changes, and section 2 above is the map of what to keep and what to rewrite. The print study is now strong enough to be the main article in its own right, with the Wikipedia study as a companion and not the lead; that reverses the order recommended on 9 November.

## 6. Status after the 23 November gaps pass

Details in `findings-2026-11-gaps-pass.md`; data in the repository (PR #4).

**Layer 3 of the thesis moves back nineteen years and gets an author.** The merger is in C.-O. Desmichels, *Tableau chronologique de l'histoire du moyen âge* (Paris: Colas, 1823), written for the history course of the Paris collèges royaux: 1202–04 fourth, Andrew II fifth, Frederick II sixth, Louis IX seventh, Tunis "huitième et dernière." Read at full text, title page viewed. **Firm.** Three years earlier the course's official outline, by Desmichels and Trognon under the Commission d'instruction publique (1820), counted seven with Frederick II fifth. A baccalauréat manual copies the 1823 headings in 1824. Rendu and Ansart (1840–42) and Bouillet (1842) are now late witnesses, and the Rendu first edition is no longer the weakest link.

Revised sentence for the article: **the crusades were numbered by dictionary compilers, a Strasbourg professor's textbook, and a Paris history teacher writing for the new Université course in 1820–23; crusade historians received the numbers and did not make them.**

## 7. Status after the 27 November session

Details are in `findings-2026-11-window-1820-23.md`. The data are on the branch `window-1820-23`, in a PR on top of #4.

**The open question of section 6, answered as far as the online record goes.** Did the Université prescribe the switch, or did Desmichels make it? Its texts in the window do not prescribe it. The 1820 outline was an official programme, one of a set registered together in April 1820, and it counts seven. The baccalauréat question table was in force under the règlement of 13 March 1821. It gives the Middle Ages its own section and asks for a "précis historique des croisades" with no numbers (as printed by Hubert, 1826). The modern eight first appears in Desmichels's *Tableau chronologique*, registered on 21 December 1822 and dated 1823 on its title page. The book belongs to a series the Université commissioned, but it carries no authorization line. The 1824 cram manual answers the exam's crusade question with his list. Another manual (Ponelle, 1826) answers it without numbers. **Probable.** The 1822 printing of the question list (BnF G-31078) is unread. If it numbers the crusades, the answer shifts to the Université.

Proposed sentence for the article: **the crusades were numbered by dictionary compilers, by a Strasbourg professor's textbook, and by a Paris history teacher who revised his own official count of 1820 when he wrote for the Université's new Middle Ages course in 1822; crusade historians received the numbers and did not make them.** This stays on hold until the author has read Desmichels 1823, pp. 63–66.
