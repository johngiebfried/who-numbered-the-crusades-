# Popular essay: narrative outline

Started 9 November 2026 from the author's dictation; revised the same afternoon after the second dictation. Claims are flagged against the project data. Format assumed: a scroll essay on the project site, like the Wikipedia story, with every chapter anchored to one object (a book, a page, a dictionary entry) and one visual from the ledger. A spoken script can be cut from it later.

## The spine

1. **Opening.** Who I am. The question I found in grad school; the only prior treatment a three-page appendix (source to be named). A decade later nobody has surveyed it. The old method (my university library, then archive.org, every book with "Crusade" in the title) and why it cannot see other languages, works that never say "crusade," or the textbook and encyclopedia chains. Say plainly that the old answer (Mombert, 1894) was wrong. That is the hook.
2. **The thesis, up front.** Three claims (wording below under Flags).
3. **The numbers as everyone knows them.** One to eight, a line each, then "and sometimes a ninth." End on the wrinkle: the Barons' Crusade falls between the Sixth and Seventh and has no number.
4. **What the list assumes.** Aimed at the Holy Land and the eastern Mediterranean; the authors' idea of the "true" crusades. Traditionalists and pluralists; the "what is a crusade" debate and the Riley-Smith definition (papal authorization, vow, indulgence, penitential war). Many Holy Land expeditions fall outside the numbers.
5. **Five problem cases, each planted here and paid off later.**
   - Three First Crusades: the People's Crusade, the main First Crusade, the Crusade of 1101.
   - Henry VI (1197): why isn't it the Fourth? For some writers it is.
   - The Barons' Crusade (1239–41): a major expedition with no number.
   - Lord Edward (1271–72): part of the Eighth, or a Ninth?
   - After 1291: Alexandria (1365), Nicopolis (1396), Varna (1443–44), and crusade indulgences granted as late as Lepanto (1571).
6. **The words.** When "crusade" became the word, and when numbers joined it. Medieval sources speak of the journey, the cross, the pilgrimage. Counting starts in the early modern universal histories and church histories and runs through Enlightenment attempts, up to Gibbon.
7. **The great historians, c. 1780–1900.** Gibbon, Michaud, Wilken, Raumer, Sybel, Röhricht, Riant, and who else makes the list. Serious academic crusade history in the nineteenth century is a continental preserve, mostly French and German. England contributes editors (Stubbs edited Third Crusade sources for the Rolls Series).
8. **The popular flood.** Michaud dominant in France. Then the British and American popular histories in English, cataloged. What inspired them, with the dedications and prefaces the author has collected. Test the Crimean War idea here.
9. **The complaint.** A modern scholar who dismisses the numbers as arbitrary, which sets the question: if the experts distrust the list, who put it there? (Name still to be supplied.)
10. **The ledger.** All the sources sorted by year, with each one's first through fifth, who cites whom, and who gets the modern numbers right and how late. This is where the reveal lands: Bouillet's *Dictionnaire* (Hachette, 1842), then Cantù by 1851, Duruy 1861, Myers 1885.
11. **Textbooks and encyclopedias.** How compilers fix a number, and what they cite. The German seven, the Dutch split, Greek writers calling 1204 the Fourth from 1845.
12. **Conclusion.** Where each count went to die, who mattered, what changed our picture of the crusades.
13. **Coda.** Wikipedia, in 22 languages: link to the separate project and the scroll story "Nine, then eight."

## Plant and pay off

Each problem case in section 5 gets its answer in the narrative.

- Three First Crusades: pays off in the Wikipedia coda (English calls the People's Crusade part of the First, German calls it a forerunner; neither language ever numbers 1101).
- Henry VI as Fourth: Maimbourg and Michaud, Saint-Allais (1816), Lefranc (1834), British popular histories into the 1870s, Robson's English Michaud (1852).
- Barons' Crusade: numbered Sixth in the *American Cyclopaedia* (1873–76) and in Swinton (1874), after Proctor; unnumbered in all modern surveys; English Wikipedia had no article until 2016.
- Edward: the English Ninth lasted in Wikipedia from 2003 to 2019, and the 2019 rename used the argument that his expedition was a late arrival to Louis's.
- Late crusades: Spanish Wikipedia's "Décima cruzada" is Alexandria 1365; Arabic has a Tenth in the war on terror; Czech numbers four crusades against the Hussites.

## What the dictation leaves out

- **Stakes.** Why a reader should care. The number turns 1204 into one item in a series and decides what "the Fifth" means.
- **The reveal named.** The outline reaches the ledger without saying what it finds. Bouillet 1842 should be the climax.
- **The receiving side.** Greek, Arabic, Hebrew and Persian use the western scheme. A short passage keeps the essay from being only about France, Germany, Britain and America.
- **A "how I know" box.** Verification levels. Most rows rest on Google Books snippets, headings or a single passage; 85 works in the dataset and 115 rows in the ledger.
- **After 1945.** A brief note on Setton, Runciman, Riley-Smith and Tyerman, in line with the plan's coda zone.

## Flags

- **Thesis 1 is wrong as worded.** Numbering is not a late eighteenth-century invention. Carion and Bullinger have twelve, Fuller (1639) thirteen "voyages," Maimbourg (1675–76) seven. Defensible: numbering is seventeenth-century and loose; the fixed modern eight dates from 1842.
- **Thesis 2 needs a definition of "popular."** Michaud was a member of the Académie française and wrote from sources. Define by genre and audience (reference books, schoolbooks, narrative histories for general readers), not by the author's status. And "academics don't number" overstates: Riant (1875) fixed the Fourth and Röhricht (1879–91) the Fifth. Wilken and Raumer are the ones who named instead.
- **Thesis 3 overreaches.** The count settled in English, French and Italian. German still has seven, Polish seven, and English Wikipedia went from nine to eight in 2019.
- **"Holy Land" is loose.** The Fourth went to Constantinople, the Fifth and Seventh to Egypt, the Eighth to Tunis.
- **"Papal bull" is wrong for the earliest crusades.** There was no bull in 1095. *Quantum praedecessores* (1145) is the first crusade bull. Say "papal authorization."
- **"Biggest unnumbered expedition" for the Barons' Crusade is contestable.** The People's Crusade and 1101 were also large. Safer: the best documented, or the one most often left out of the series.
- **The Crimean War cannot explain the origin of the eight.** Bouillet is 1842; the war is 1853–56. It might explain a boom in English popular histories in the 1850s and later. Test it against dates and dedications before using it.
- **Tyerman and Riley-Smith are allies, not opponents.** The standard split is traditionalists against pluralists. Verify attributions against the books.
- **Stubbs** is an editor of sources, not a crusade historian. The point still works as a sign of how thin English academic work was.
