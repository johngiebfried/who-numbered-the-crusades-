# November 2026 review: findings

Summary of the fact-check of the 2022 *Crusades* submission and the research passes that followed (8 November 2026). The full review with quotations and links is the Claude Doc "Who Numbered the Crusades? Fact-check and evidence review" (main tab and "Numbering corpus" tab), now migrated into `data/`.

## Verdict

- The title answer fails, by more than fifty years. Mombert (1894) was not the first to use the modern one-to-eight sequence. Bouillet's *Dictionnaire universel d'histoire et de géographie* (Paris: Hachette, 1842) has it: "On compte généralement huit croisades," fourth 1202–04, fifth under Honorius III (1218), sixth 1228–29 under Frederick II, Tunis eighth. Cantù's *Storia universale* adopted the same list by 1851. Duruy (1861) and Myers (1885) have it too; Myers notes that counting Henry VI's expedition "the number increased to nine."
- Near-modern eights were in English before Mombert: the *American Cyclopaedia* (1873–76) and Swinton (1874) make the Barons' Crusade the sixth.
- Numbering is early modern: Fuller (1639) thirteen "voyages," Maimbourg (1675–76) seven, Carion and Bullinger twelve (Constable 2001, 17), Saint-Allais (1816) eight with Henry VI fourth.
- The modern eight looks like a synthesis: Gibbon's fourth and fifth plus the sixth to eighth of French school counts (Lefranc 1834).
- Ngram: "Sixth," "Seventh," "Eighth Crusade" combined, 12.0 per billion words in 1850–89 and 8.4 in 1890–1949; "Fourth Crusade" rose from 17.8 to 31.5. No 1894–1951 "golden age."
- What survives of the paper: its table where checked; popular histories as the least standardized genre; scholarship fixing the Fourth (Riant 1875) and Fifth (Röhricht 1879–91, a number Gibbon had already given Damietta); Barker's 1923 *Crusades* as a reprint of EB11.

## Who read whom

- Two English-language lines, both with Gibbon's Fourth (ch. LIX, "the Fourth and Fifth Crusades"; Guizot's French Gibbon 1812). Maimbourg and Michaud made Henry VI's expedition the fourth; Michaud's preface to Cottin's *Mathilde* calls 1202–04 "la cinquième."
- Myers's preface names Michaud as a principal authority and Gibbon as a lesser one; his chapter cites Michaud at least six times. Robson's English Michaud (1852) still has Henry VI fourth.
- Pre-1885 American eight: Mills's count with Gibbon's Fourth, carried by Proctor (d. 1842; printed 1854). Proctor's sixth is the Barons' Crusade; the *American Cyclopaedia* lists him and prints his count; Swinton matches.
- Duruy cites no modern historian; Bouillet, from the same publisher, is the likeliest source (inference).

## German

- Encyclopedias of the 1830s–60s count six and skip 1202–04 (Damen, Brockhaus 1837, Pierer: "gewöhnlich gar nicht gerechnet"). Herder numbers nothing. Brockhaus 1911 has seven.
- Schoolbooks from the 1840s (Pütz, Weber, Ploetz) count 1202–04 fourth, Frederick II fifth, Louis IX sixth and seventh: Gibbon's seven with Frederick II in Damietta's place. Translations reached English readers; D. S. Schaff (1907) uses the German count.
- Academics named rather than numbered. GND headings are dated, with "Fünfter Kreuzzug" a variant for Damietta. German Wikipedia and a 2018 classroom unit still count seven.

## Italian, Spanish, Greek, Dutch, French

- Cantù's first edition (1842–44): "Quarta e quinta Crociata" (1202–04; Andrew II's 1217 expedition fifth), "Sesta Crociata" (Damietta), "Settima e ottava" (Louis IX). By 1851 the same text is re-split to Bouillet's list. His bibliography names Wilken and Michaud.
- Michaud was translated in Milan, Florence, Naples, Venice and reprinted by Sonzogno 1878–1941; Italian books before 1890 mix every count; the eight takes over Italian school texts in the 1890s.
- French before Bouillet: Lefranc (1834) eight with Henry VI fourth; Lévi Alvarès (1835) seven, refusing to count Frederick II's voyage as a crusade.
- Spanish: the Reconquista stays outside the series (Las Navas unnumbered between the Fourth and Fifth). Avendaño (1846) has an eight; Milà i Fontanals (1849) the modern fourth and sixth.
- Greek: 1204 is "Τετάρτη Σταυροφορία" from Koromelas (1845); the 1893 teacher-college syllabus pairs it with the fall of medieval Hellenism.
- Dutch splits: northern schoolbooks the German seven; French-derived and Catholic books the French eight.
