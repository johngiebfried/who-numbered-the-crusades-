## 1. Verdict

## 2. New findings

### 2.1 "Eight" is an eighteenth-century dictionary total (verified in part)

- *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, 1st ed. (1771), vol. 2, s.v. CROISADE: "They reckon eight croisades for the conquest of the holy land; the first begun in the year 1095." The other seven are not listed. https://jimclifford.ca/early_encyclopedia_britannica/articles/1771/eb_1st_1771_005061.html (**verified**)
- Saint-Allais (1816), already in the corpus, lists the eight in full: 1096; [second]; 1188; fourth 1195; fifth 1199; sixth 1217; seventh 1248; eighth 1270. https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Dictionnaire_encyclop%C3%A9dique_de_la_noblesse_de_France_-_volume_1_-_Nicolas_Viton_de_Saint-Allais.djvu/234 (**verified**)
- EB1's wording probably comes from Chambers's *Cyclopaedia* (1728), and the same list probably stands in Trévoux and the *Encyclopédie* (1754). (**recalled**; the Wikisource *Encyclopédie* page would not load. Check Furetière 1690, Trévoux, Moréri, Chambers, *Encyclopédie*, EB1–7.)

Consequence. The total of eight, with Louis IX as seventh and eighth, was fixed in reference books at least 71 years before Bouillet. Bouillet's own "On compte généralement" reports a custom. What changed between Saint-Allais/Lefranc and Bouillet is slots four to six: Henry VI (1195) dropped out and Frederick II (1228) came in. Lévi Alvarès refusing Frederick II in 1835 is that fight in progress. Phase 1 should be restated: not "who first had the eight" but "who dropped Henry VI and seated Frederick II inside an existing eight, between c. 1816 and 1842." This replaces the "Gibbon's fourth and fifth plus Lefranc's sixth to eighth" inference with something simpler and testable.

Hypothesis to test (**judgment**): Bouillet was a school administrator and his dictionary was written for the Université market; the official history programmes of the 1830s–40s and the collège *précis* written to them are the likeliest place for the reshuffle.

Not re-verified tonight: the Bouillet 1842 quotation itself. Search did not reach a scan.

### 2.2 The Catholic Encyclopedia is the bridge from print to Wikipedia

- The paper's table dates the *Catholic Encyclopedia* to 1917. The article is by Louis Bréhier, vol. 4, 1908. https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04543c.htm (**verified**)
- Bréhier, a French Byzantinist, opens: "It has been customary to describe the Crusades as eight in number," lists them, and calls the division "arbitrary." His dates: 1095–1101, 1145–47, 1188–92, 1204, 1217, 1228–29 (with 1239 under the sixth), 1249–52, 1270. (**verified**)
- Those are the eight date ranges recorded in `findings-2026-11-wikipedia-2001-and-911.md` for the oldest surviving English Wikipedia version (28 October 2001, "no sources"). Eight of eight, including the odd ones (1095–1101, 1188–92, 1249–52). (**verified** against the project note; the revision itself would not load from here. Compare wording in rev 349190657 with the New Advent text.)

If the wording check holds, the 2001 article was the Catholic Encyclopedia's list, and three weeks later Schaff-Herzog was pasted beside it. The first Wikipedia page was two public-domain encyclopedias of 1908, the French eight and the German seven, on one screen. That documents the link the sub-project brief only asserts ("modern descendant"). It also kills the 2022 paper's "simultaneous creation in English": the English eight of 1908–11 has a French author on one side.

## 3. Errata in the 2022 paper (for anything reused)

All **recalled** unless marked; check each against a title page.

1. Fuller's *Historie of the Holy Warre* is 1639, not 1631. He never held a Cambridge chair.
2. Maimbourg was expelled from the Jesuits c. 1681–82; the *Histoire des Croisades* is 1675–76. He did not write it at Saint-Victor.
3. Mills: first edition 1820 (text says 1822, bibliography 1828).
4. Gibbon: the crusade chapters are in vol. 6 (1788), not vol. 4.
5. Michelet's *Les Croisades* (1880) is a posthumous extract from the *Histoire de France* (1833); he died in 1874. He belongs before Bouillet, not after Kugler.
6. Cox, *The Crusades*: 1874. EB9 vol. 6: 1877.
7. *Catholic Encyclopedia*: 1908, Bréhier (**verified**).
8. *Historians' History of the World*: first published 1904 (table 1908, bibliography 1907).
9. Stubbs was Regius Professor (1866–84), then Bishop of Chester (1884), then of Oxford (1889). Not both at once. *Seventeen Lectures*: 1886.
10. "Proctor": British catalogs give George Procter. He died in 1842 (project note), so the 1854 Crimean War preface is the publisher's, not his. That is evidence for war-driven reprinting of an older text, not for war-driven writing.
11. Munro's "Fourth Crusade" in *Translations and Reprints* is 1896, not 1907.
12. Barker as Smail's doctoral supervisor: not confirmed. Check Edbury's wording.
13. Spellings: Recueil, Poujoulat, Prutz, Kugler's *Geschichte der Kreuzzüge*, Mechanics' Institutes; "inexorably linked" should be "inextricably."
14. "Stevenson is the first historian to use the standard academic way": an earliest-found claim, same trap as Mombert.

Project-doc inconsistencies: the scope note says the German box has "nothing north of the Elbe" and also lists the Wendish Crusade since 2005; the sub-project brief still calls Wikipedia "the most-read crusade reference"; the ledger (78 print works, coded from the Claude Doc) and `works.csv` (85) are two sources of truth; the scope tables are "not yet saved as files"; nothing is committed.

## 4. Method (judgment)

1. **Earliest-found is a terminus ante quem.** Say "attested by" and put the weight on diffusion: share of works per decade using each scheme, by genre and country.
2. **The corpus is a string-search sample.** Searching for ordinals finds works that number. It cannot support "dominant," "most" or shares. Either restrict claims to existence and chains, or build one real sampling frame (German schoolbooks via GEI-Digital; French schoolbooks via a textbook catalog) and count inside it.
3. **Use the Wikipedia design on print.** `stated_count` ("on compte huit") and slot assignments are separate measures there and merged in the print tables. EB1 1771 is a stated eight with no slots; the codebook cannot hold it.
4. **Count, label, name.** The Arabic move from indefinite to definite titles is the project's best concept. Add a field for the function of an ordinal: enumeration in running text, heading, proper name.
5. **Verification.** Page-verify at full text the fifteen or so rows that carry the argument (the dictionary chain, Lefranc, Lévi Alvarès, Bouillet, Cantù 1844/1851, Duruy, Myers, Bréhier) before any more breadth. Tier 3–4 languages can wait.
6. **Disclosure.** The corpus was found and coded with an LLM in long sessions. A *Digital Medievalist* methods section has to say so and say what a human checked. Until then the review doc's rows are leads.

Recommendation: B, with the popular essay held until the dictionary chain is checked, because its climax (Bouillet) is about to move.

Thesis wording for the essay, replacing the three dictated claims: (1) counting is old and loose, and dictionaries were saying "eight" by 1771 before anyone agreed which eight; (2) reference books and schoolbooks, not crusade historians, decided which expeditions fill the slots, in France, by 1842; (3) it never fully settled: Germany kept seven, and Wikipedia went from nine to eight in 2019.
