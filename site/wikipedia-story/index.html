Who Numbered the Crusades? · Wikipedia, 2001–2026

Nine, then eight

How the free encyclopedia counted the crusades, sent one count around the world in twenty languages, argued for twenty years over what a crusade is, and then quietly took a number back.

Pope Urban II at the Council of Clermont, 1095, as Jean Colombe painted it about 1474 (Passages d'outremer, BnF Français 5594). Wikimedia Commons, public domain. Its Commons file was uploaded by Adam Bishop, the editor who built much of English Wikipedia's crusade coverage.

Ask anyone how many crusades there were and you will get a number: eight, or nine. The number feels like part of the history. It isn't. Nobody who took the cross knew which crusade they were on. The numbers were added centuries later, by printers, schoolmasters and encyclopedists, and they never agreed. German readers learned seven, French readers eight, some English readers nine, and one seventeenth-century Englishman counted thirteen.

Then, in 2001, a new kind of encyclopedia started writing the crusades down, one edit at a time, in public. Every change it ever made is still on file. This is what that record shows.

OpeningOctober 2001

The first draft

The oldest version of the English article "Crusades" that survives is dated 28 October 2001, and signed by a user called Alan Millar. It runs to about 4,000 characters. It has no footnotes and no sources, and it lists people the way a card index does: under the Second Crusade, a single name; under "General links", King Alphonso VIII of Spain, followed by an honest question: which crusade?

Eight crusades

It counts eight, in bold, one after another. The Fifth is 1217, with Andrew II of Hungary. The Sixth is Frederick II's, 1228–29. The Seventh and Eighth are Louis IX's. There is no Ninth.

The Children's Crusade of 1212 comes after the list, "not really a crusade in the same sense." The draft has already started deciding what counts.

Two counts on one page

Three weeks later, on 19 November, Millar pasted in a long article from an old encyclopedia, "to integrate": the New Schaff-Herzog Encyclopedia of Religious Knowledge (1908–14), an American work built on a German one. Its table of contents ran "The Fifth, Sixth, and Seventh Crusades, 1228–70."

That is the German count of the nineteenth century. It skips Damietta, so Frederick II is the Fifth. For four and a half months, until April 2002, the article called the same expedition the Sixth near the top and the Fifth near the bottom.

Seven weeks after 9/11

That first surviving draft was saved forty-seven days after the attacks of 11 September. It has a section called "Reputation and evaluation." Islamic fundamentalists, it says, use the word "crusade" for "Western moves against them." Then it adds a thought of its own: an "interesting symmetry" between crusade and jihad, each a good word at home and a bad one abroad.

Whether Millar wrote that before or after September 11 is unknowable: earlier versions are lost. The article never names the attacks, in any yearly sample from 2002 to 2012.

And then the moment passes

The jihad paragraph lasted until April 2004. The sentence on fundamentalists was gone by mid-2005. President Bush's own use of "crusade," on 16 September 2001, entered the article only around 2008, misdated to 2002, and was gone again by mid-2009.

For a topic suddenly on everyone's lips, the article spent the early war on terror saying remarkably little about it.

No spike

Nor did the attacks bring editors. For two and a half years the article was edited a handful of times a month, never more than nine. The crowd arrived in 2005 and peaked at — edits in March 2007.

The pattern of that growth follows the school calendar more than the news: busy in the autumn and spring terms, quieter each summer. The people shaping the article look less like readers of headlines than like students and their teachers.

The box

On 2 April 2004 an editor called Itai made a small navigation box to sit at the side of every crusade article. It had — links: the First to the Ninth, and three more, the Albigensian, Children's and Northern crusades.

That box is the hero of this story. It numbered nine from its first day, and it was about to be copied, link for link, into language after language.

Act I

The numbers

How a Ninth was made, exported and taken back

Gustave Doré, Peter the Hermit Preaching the Crusade, engraving for Michaud's Histoire des croisades (1877). Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

A Ninth, made in 2003

The Ninth came first to the article, not the box. On 13 April 2003 English Wikipedia gave Edward I's expedition of 1271–72 a heading of its own; in May 2004 it got an article, "Ninth Crusade." From 2004 to 2012 the article told its readers: "A traditional numbering scheme for the crusades gives us nine."

It was less traditional than that. In the printed books, a Ninth for Edward is rare, and even then it is a Ninth counted differently, with Tunis and Edward together.

Copied, language by language

The route is sometimes visible to the day. Five weeks after the English Ninth appeared, the editor who had split up the English article added "Negende Kruistocht" to Dutch. Portuguese had a full set of nine by 2005, Italian by 2008, Hebrew's box was headed "the nine great crusades" in 2009, Spanish switched from eight to nine in 2020.

No earlier medium had done this. In print, every country kept its own count for two hundred years. On Wikipedia, thirteen of these fifteen editions came to list nine.

The holdouts

Arabic's box counted eight for nine years and became the last to move, on 4 December 2013. German never moved. Its box still reads "Fünfter (Sechster) Kreuzzug": the old German seven first, the modern count in brackets. When someone made Edward the "Eighth" in 2010, a German editor reversed it and explained, on the talk page, that one-to-nine was the English count.

Polish counts seven by another route: it has no article on Frederick II's crusade at all.

And English takes it back

On 17 November 2019 an English editor argued that Edward's expedition was a late arrival to Louis IX's crusade, not a crusade of its own. Nobody objected. "Ninth Crusade" became "Lord Edward's crusade," and the Ninth left the box the same day. By the end of 2021 the main article said eight.

Persian copied the rename three months later and undid it the next day. Every other edition that took up the Ninth still has it. The number English Wikipedia spread is now kept by everyone except English.

Readers still type "Ninth"

English Wikipedia is about half of all reading of these articles, so its count is the one most people meet. In 2018 the "Ninth Crusade" article had — views. In 2025, six years after the rename, the old title, now only a redirect, still brought in — readers.

An encyclopedia can drop a number. Its readers keep looking for it.

Act II

The box

Twenty years of arguing over what a crusade is

Gustave Doré, Saint Louis arrive devant Damiette, for Michaud's Histoire des croisades (1877), detail. Bibliothèque nationale de France via Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

A box that keeps growing

Once the numbers settled, the fight moved to the edges. The English box grew from — links to over thirty by 2011. Some additions were tentative ("just testing, can be removed," wrote the editor who added the People's Crusade in 2004). Some were reversed within weeks: the Reconquista went out in 2006 as "not really a Crusade in the formal sense."

In December 2010 the numbered crusades were set in bold, to stand out from the crowd now around them.

2016: "template creep"

In June 2016 the box got headings for the first time, sorted by enemy: the Holy Land, pagans, Muslims, heretics. Six months later another editor cut nine links, including the Reconquista and Nicopolis, with a rule: "only list things actually called 'crusades.'" On the talk page he called the growth "template creep."

The rule sounds simple. Every argument since has been about what it means.

2021: a peak and a purge

In October 2021 new sections arrived in a rush: ideology and institutions, Italian crusades, the Holy Leagues of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Within weeks a six-point cleanup took out most of it, including every popular crusade. One edit summary named the problem: "some articles with crusade in the very title are not in the box."

2025: 107 links, then 64

In April 2025 the box peaked at — links. For eleven days it had a section called "Reactionary Crusades (1789–1939)": the Vendée, the Carlist Wars, Mexico's Cristero War, the Spanish Civil War. Then came the cut: the box was "not supposed to be repository of all things 'Crusade' but the list of important campaigns." It ended the year at —.

German, for comparison, went from — links in 2005 to — today, in one chronological line, and has barely been touched.

In, out, in, out

Some entries never settle. The Reconquista has entered the English box four times and left three. Since October 2025 it is back, renamed "Iberian Crusades (1095–1492)," which makes the case for including it in the label itself.

Where does it end?

The early modern wars against the Ottomans (Lepanto, Vienna 1683) and the Spanish Armada have gone in and out six times, on the question of whether a crusade can happen after the Middle Ages. A "Proto-crusades" heading lasted three weeks in 2022 before an editor noted that "historians find this a debatable term." The popular crusades were dropped in 2021 and restored in 2023.

These are real questions in the scholarship. On Wikipedia they are settled, and unsettled, by whoever edited last.

The numbers are now the minority

Here is the result in the Holy Land section of the English box today: — expeditions, of which only — carry a number. The Crusade of 1101, the Barons' Crusade, Henry VI's crusade of 1197: each got a date, a place or a leader, and none got a number, in any language.

Wikipedia froze the numbered series just as it started listing everything around it.

Act III

Twenty-two Wikipedias

Each language draws the line in its own place

Bayezid I routs the crusaders at Nicopolis, 1396, from Lokman's Hünernâme (1588), detail. Wikimedia Commons, public domain. English Wikipedia has put Nicopolis in its crusade box, taken it out, and put it back.

One box, twenty-two answers

Almost every Wikipedia has a crusade box, and every revision of every one is on record. Linking each entry to the same Wikidata item lets us line them up. The numbered crusades are nearly universal. Everything else varies: English lists — expeditions, Czech —, Polish —, Swedish —.

English is the main exporter. Spanish (2023), Arabic, Persian and Ukrainian copied its sections, sometimes down to labels like "1150," "1249" and "1293" for the Swedish crusades.

Spain and the Reconquista

You might expect Spanish Wikipedia to make the Reconquista a crusade. It doesn't, much: one link, added in 2012. The most Iberian box is Catalan, which has listed the conquest of Majorca since 2010 and in August 2026 relabeled the Crown of Aragon's wars as crusades, one by one: "Croada de Barbastre," "Croada d'Almeria."

On 17 July 2018 a single anonymous edit added Guernica and the bombing of Barcelona to the Catalan box, probably after Franco's own "Cruzada." It was gone the same day.

The north

The Danish box began in 2009 not with Jerusalem but with the Teutonic Order and the Albigensian crusade; the numbered crusades arrived only in 2013. Finnish went the other way. In 2005 someone added three crusades to Finland; in 2007 an editor removed them: "I am ruthlessly removing those three crusades to Finland."

Meanwhile English titles the same three expeditions the First, Second and Third Swedish Crusades. Wikipedia numbers in the north what it refuses to number in the Levant.

Arabic, Persian, Hebrew

Do the editions in the languages of the crusaders' Muslim opponents leave out the crusades Europeans fought among themselves? They do not. Arabic added the Cathars and the Hussites in 2017 and calls its Reconquista section "the Fall of al-Andalus." Persian now keeps three political crusades, among them the Spanish Armada, that English had just removed.

The narrow one is Hebrew: twenty links, and nothing after 1291.

National crusades

Editors put their own country's crusades in. Czech numbers its own series, the only box that numbers anything outside the canonical nine: the First, Second, Third and Fourth Crusades against the Hussites (1420–31). Dutch adds Drenthe and the Stedinger peasants. Portuguese adds its wars with the Ottomans.

Turkish, briefly, went furthest: from 2008 to 2010 its box listed Lepanto and the siege of Vienna in 1683 as crusades.

And leave their own out

Just as often, an edition leaves out the crusade that happened on its own ground. Polish has never listed the Teutonic crusades. Russian has no Battle on the Ice. Hungarian has no Varna, Portuguese no Lisbon, Finnish no Finland.

Hebrew lists the Shepherds' Crusade of 1251 but not that of 1320, the one that attacked Jewish communities.

Coda

The Tenth

Where 9/11 finally shows up

The Psalter World Map, about 1265, with Jerusalem at its centre (British Library, Add MS 28681), detail. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

September 11 left almost no mark on Wikipedia's medieval crusades. It shows up somewhere else: in the attempts to add one more.

English, 2005–2010

A "Tenth Crusade" page existed for five years before it was deleted.

Arabic, since 2009

"The Tenth Crusade" is an article about the US-led war on terror, the phrase used as a political metaphor. It is the only live Tenth in any edition.

Spanish, since 2018

"Décima cruzada" redirects to Peter I of Cyprus's attack on Alexandria in 1365, a medieval candidate no print tradition numbered.

The numbers were never the crusaders' own. For two centuries they belonged to national publishing traditions, which disagreed. Wikipedia was the first to give the whole world one count, and it did so fast, by copying. Then the edition that started it changed its mind, while twenty others kept what it had taught them. And readers still type "Ninth Crusade" into the search box.

Who numbered the crusades? On Wikipedia, we can name them.
