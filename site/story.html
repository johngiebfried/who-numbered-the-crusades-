The life of a number

Everyone knows there were eight crusades, or nine. The numbers came centuries after the crusades, and they were never settled. This story follows four of them through printed books and library catalogs since the sixteenth century and through Wikipedia since 2002. Each dot on the chart is a source; the number inside is the one it gives.

Chapter 1 · The Ninth

A crusade with no number

Edward's crusade of 1271–72 was a late arrival to Louis IX's expedition to Tunis. No book in the corpus gives it a number of its own. Most leave it out or fold it into Tunis. Mills (1820) called the two together "the Last Crusade"; the American Cyclopaedia and Myers counted them as one Eighth.

Where Edward does carry a Ninth (Cox in 1874, the ninth Britannica in 1877, Newman in 1899), it is still Tunis and Edward together, in a count that started with Henry VI's crusade. That Ninth is the modern Eighth.

Wikipedia makes it the Ninth

English Wikipedia gave Edward a Ninth heading on 13 April 2003 and an article of his own in May 2004. Five weeks after that heading, the editor who had split the English article added a Ninth to Dutch Wikipedia.

Of the fifteen editions on this chart, thirteen came to list a Ninth in their navigation boxes. Arabic's box, at eight since 2004, added one in December 2013. German and Polish never did.

And English unmakes it

On 17 November 2019 English Wikipedia renamed "Ninth Crusade" to "Lord Edward's crusade" and dropped the number from its navigation box the same day. Persian copied the rename in February 2020 and reverted it the next day.

Every other edition here that took up the Ninth still has it. The convention English Wikipedia spread is now kept by the editions that copied it, and not by English.

Readers still look for it

As an article, "Ninth Crusade" drew about — views in 2018. As a redirect it still drew — in 2025, about a third of all the traffic to Edward's crusade.

The rename changed the page. It did not change what people type.

Chapter 2 · Two Fifths

Damietta: Fourth, Fifth or Sixth

The siege of Damietta (1217–21) is the modern Fifth, and Gibbon already called it that in 1788. The German encyclopedias of the 1830s to 1860s made it the Fourth, because they did not count 1202–04 at all. Michaud and the books that followed him made it the Sixth, because they counted Henry VI's crusade of 1197.

One expedition, three numbers, each consistent with its own list.

Frederick II takes the Fifth

Frederick II's crusade (1228–29) is the modern Sixth. German encyclopedias and schoolbooks made it the Fifth: Weber and Ploetz count 1202–04 Fourth and go straight to Frederick. Their English translations carried that count to English-speaking readers, and D. S. Schaff still used it in 1907.

German Wikipedia's navigation box keeps it today, with the modern number in brackets: "Fünfter (Sechster) Kreuzzug." The German National Library files both as variants of a dated heading.

Chapter 3 · The crusade without a number

The Barons' Crusade

The expeditions of Theobald of Navarre and Richard of Cornwall (1239–41) once had numbers. Proctor, the American Cyclopaedia and Swinton made them the Sixth; Mills, Perry and Cox the Seventh; Fuller in 1639 the Tenth. The Catholic Encyclopedia folded them into Frederick's Sixth.

The eleventh Britannica (1911) named the expedition and gave it no number, and the modern list followed. English Wikipedia had no page on it until February 2016, and no edition has ever given it a number.

Chapter 4 · How many?

Five to thirteen

Totals ran from five to thirteen. Fuller counted thirteen voyages, Carion and Bullinger twelve, Maimbourg seven, Müller five. In the nineteenth century German encyclopedias settled on six, German schoolbooks on seven, French reference books on eight, and several English popular histories on nine.

Wikipedia briefly gave the world one answer, nine, and English Wikipedia has since dropped it. Nobody has made a Tenth stick: Spanish Wikipedia points "Décima cruzada" at Peter I of Cyprus's attack on Alexandria in 1365, and Arabic Wikipedia uses "the Tenth Crusade" for the war on terror.

Where this comes from

Every dot is a row in the crusade numbering ledger: the printed works and catalogs of the project's numbering corpus and the crusade navigation boxes of fifteen Wikipedias, sampled each July. The full grid, with every expedition and every source, is the companion ledger page.

Reading the chart

The time axis is stretched where the sources are dense (1780–1930 and 2002–26) and compressed between. Wikipedia runs are drawn as bars, one row per language, from the July sample where a numbering first appears to the last sample where it holds. A blank means the source as checked gives no number, not that it is silent. Chapter 4 shows only sources that state a total, and for Wikipedia the highest number in the box.
