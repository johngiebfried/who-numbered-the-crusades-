# Wikipedia numbering: first and second passes

8 November 2026. Ten editions sampled yearly (en, de, fr, it, es, nl, el; ar, fa, he), plus titles and navboxes in nine more (tr, ru, pt, sv, pl, cs, hu, da, no), 2001 to the present. Second pass 9 November: pageviews, alternative numbers in article leads, Wikidata, GND, talk archives. Data in `data/wikipedia/`, chart in `site/wikipedia-timeline.html`.

## The Barons' Crusade lead

Confirmed, and later than remembered. English Wikipedia had no page of any kind called "Barons' Crusade" until 12 February 2016, when Blue Danube created it as a full article (rev 704652276). The title has no earlier history: no redirect, no deletion, no move into it. The "Crusade of 1239" redirect dates from the same day, and the article went into the navigation box one minute later, unnumbered (rev 704652443).

Before that the expedition was barely present. The main "Crusades" article first named it on 1 May 2013 (Ealdgyth, rev 553107724), in one sentence presenting Theobald I of Navarre's expedition as a follow-up to the Sixth Crusade. From 2003 to 2013 the English article carried a Ninth Crusade and no 1239.

English was not first. French had an article in August 2008 ("Croisade de 1239", renamed "Croisade des barons" in 2016), German in October 2010, Spanish in June 2018 (after a 2014 redirect that pointed at an unrelated Albigensian article), Italian in September 2022, Dutch in September 2023 and Greek in March 2026. No edition gives it a number. The American reference books of the 1870s that made it the Sixth (the *American Cyclopaedia*, Swinton) left no trace here.

## What the counts show

German Wikipedia aside, the editions converged on nine between 2003 and 2012 in their lists and navigation boxes (the Spanish main article held out until 2020). They did it faster and more uniformly than any printed tradition this project has found. English led and is now the one that has retreated.

**English.** The single "Crusade" article of 2002 counted eight, with dated entries from First to Eighth. On 17 October 2002 Andre Engels split the First to Fourth into their own articles; "Eighth Crusade" began that day as a redirect. A Ninth heading appears on 13 April 2003 (rev 1093670). Adam Bishop created "Ninth Crusade" on 4 May 2004 and in July 2004 added a sentence that, from 2007 reworded to "totals nine," ran until 2012: "A traditional numbering scheme for the crusades gives us nine." The navigation box counted First to Ninth from its creation in April 2004.

The retreat came in three steps. In November 2019 Srnec proposed renaming "Ninth Crusade" to "Lord Edward's crusade," arguing that Edward's expedition was a late arrival to Louis IX's Tunis crusade, not a separate event. Nobody objected, the move went through on 17 November, and Srnec dropped "Ninth" from the navigation box the same day (rev 926657250). Johnbod moved the page back on 18 September 2020, calling the speedy close improper; Srnec restored the new title within hours. The main article's lead switched to "eight major numbered crusades" in June 2021, and the last "Ninth Crusade" left it on 22 December 2021 (rev 1061598911). By mid-2024 the lead gave no count, and by mid-2026 a footnote pointed readers to the "List of Crusades" (created December 2020), which says "First through Eighth or Ninth."

The English nine lasted eighteen years in the main article (2003–21) and fifteen as an article title (2004–19). In March 2008 a comment on its talk page asked the question this project could have written: what source in English lists it as ninth?

**German.** The first German article (2002) numbered one to six in the modern order, with the Fifth 1217–18 and the Sixth 1228. In March 2004 Hubert22 replaced this with a double count: "(Fünfter) Kreuzzug" for Damietta, "Fünfter (Sechster) Kreuzzug" for Frederick II, then "Sechster (Siebter)" and "Siebter (Achter)" for Louis IX (rev 938521). That is the nineteenth-century German schoolbook seven (Pütz, Weber, Ploetz), with the modern count in brackets. In February 2007 both Fifth Crusade articles lost their numbers ("Kreuzzug von Damiette," "Kreuzzug Friedrichs II."), and "Fünfter Kreuzzug" became a disambiguation page. In April 2007 Rolz Reus removed the brackets from the main article and wrote that historians count "insgesamt sieben Kreuzzüge … als offizielle Kreuzzüge" (rev 30022882). That sentence is still there, with Damietta and Frederick II grouped under one Fifth. The navigation box has kept the bracketed double count since 2005.

Edward's crusade reached German Wikipedia in September 2010 as "Achter Kreuzzug," for one day. Herrgott renamed it because an Eighth was "in der Literatur unüblich (eher 7. oder 9.)." On the talk page he set out the alternative (Damietta Fifth, Frederick Sixth, Louis IX Seventh and Eighth, Edward Ninth) and called it the "englische" count. "Achter Kreuzzug" now redirects to Tunis, the Siebter, and "Neunter Kreuzzug" to Edward. A German editor in 2010 thus identified the one-to-nine sequence as English, and German Wikipedia still prints the modern count only as the bracketed alternative.

**French.** Nine from the start (main article 2004, "Neuvième croisade" December 2004, both navigation boxes). The main article's text dropped to eight from 2009 to 2021, said "neuf" from December 2021, and since December 2025 says eight main crusades, "parfois étendues à neuf" with Edward's expedition. Tunis stays the Eighth throughout, as in Bouillet.

**Italian.** Nine in the main article and navigation box from 2008; "Nona crociata" exists from December 2005. The Barons' Crusade is in the navigation box from 2022 (as "1239") but has never appeared in the main article.

**Spanish.** The main article said "Hubo ocho cruzadas" from 2003 to September 2020, while linking a "Novena cruzada" from 2017. It switched to nine on 4 September 2020 (rev 129019369), with a section headed "Las nueve cruzadas." Spanish is the only edition with a Tenth: since 2018 "Décima cruzada" redirects to Peter I of Cyprus's Alexandria crusade of 1365. That is the expedition the codebook already flags as a candidate.

**Dutch.** Nine from May 2003, and the route is documented. On 21 May 2003 Andre Engels, who had split the English article seven months earlier, added "Achtste Kruistocht (1270)" and "Negende Kruistocht (1271–1272)" to the Dutch article (rev 44312), five weeks after the English Ninth appeared. The nineteenth-century Dutch split between the northern German seven and the Catholic and French eight left no mark.

**Greek.** The first article (March 2005) was a bare list of links from Α΄ to Ζ΄, and the navigation box grew from seven (2005) to eight (by December 2006) to nine (by November 2007). Greek had no article on Edward's crusade until February 2019. The main article's passage on numbering (from 2015) translates a passage from the English article on merged and split counts, down to its "sometimes" Ninth. The project's Greek finding still holds: 1204 is the Δ΄ in every sampled year.

## Arabic, Persian and Hebrew

These three matter because they are the languages of the people on the receiving end of the crusades, and of Jerusalem. All three numbered the crusades on the western scheme from the start; none kept or invented a count of its own. Each took up the Ninth at a different time.

**Arabic counted eight for nearly a decade.** One editor, Uwe a, built the main article, the navigation box (September 2004) and most numbered articles in 2004–05, and the box listed First to Eighth. The Ninth arrived on 30 October 2010, when an anonymous editor created the Ninth Crusade article and added it to the main article the same day (rev 5949534). The navigation box held at eight until 4 December 2013 (Zaher.Kadour, rev 12091964), the last navigation box in the set to move to nine, ten years after English made the change. Arabic titles began in the indefinite form ("حملة صليبية أولى", "a first crusade") and moved to the definite between 2008 and 2018. One editor justified a move with "اسم علم لا ينكر", a proper name that cannot be indefinite. The numbers had become names. Arabic is also the only edition with a live article on a "Tenth Crusade" (2009): the phrase as a metaphor linking the US-led war on terror to the medieval crusades. The English article on the same topic was deleted. In Arabic the series continues into the present as political language.

**Persian was built in a week, and copied the English rename.** Apart from scattered earlier articles (Third 2009, Second 2013, First 2013, Fourth 2014), one editor created the Fifth to Ninth between 11 and 15 March 2016, and the navigation box appeared mid-run (13 March). Its Ninth slot linked to the English "Ninth Crusade" article. On 11 February 2020, three months after the English rename, the Persian Ninth was retitled "جنگ صلیبی ادوارد یکم" (Edward I's crusade) and restored the next day by the same editor. It is the one case in the data of an English numbering change reaching another edition, and it did not stick.

**Hebrew states nine but does not use it.** The navigation box, created in May 2009, heads its list "תשעת המסעות הגדולים", "the nine great crusades", and still does. That makes it the clearest statement of nine in any edition. The main article listed nine in 2005 but has since been rebuilt by theme (terminology, the crusades to the Land of Israel, the Albigensian and Baltic crusades, effects on the Jewish community) and links only First to Sixth. Hebrew had a Ninth Crusade article (2010) five years before an Eighth (2015). The Barons' Crusade was a redirect from 2014 and became an article only on 28 December 2025.

**Barons' Crusade.** Persian (March 2019) and Arabic (July 2019) wrote articles before Italian, Dutch, Greek or Hebrew. None numbers it.

## Second pass (9 November 2026)

### Readership

Pageviews (human readers, July 2015 to October 2026; `pageviews_annual.csv`, `pageviews_by_language.csv`) settle whose count matters. Across the ten editions, English accounts for 52% of all views of these articles in 2016–25, Spanish 14%, German 9%, French 8%. The English "Crusades" article alone drew 1.5 to 2.1 million views a year. The German seven, the one dissenting count among large editions, reaches under a tenth of readers. For most people who look up a crusade on Wikipedia, the count is the English one: nine until 2019, eight since.

The rename did not change what readers search for. The English redirect "Ninth Crusade" still drew 27,363 views in 2025, a third of all traffic to Edward's crusade (82,000). In 2019, before the move, it had about 104,000. Readers still arrive looking for a Ninth.

This does not prove the brief's claim that Wikipedia is "the most-read crusade reference"; no comparable figures exist for Britannica or print. It does establish the scale. Safe wording for the article: "read millions of times a year".

### Alternative numbers in the articles themselves

The yearly opening paragraphs of the Fifth to Ninth articles (`alternative_number_statements.csv`) show where each edition admits another count:

- The English Eighth carried "sometimes counted as the Seventh" from 2005 until 2022. Arabic translated the sentence in 2006 and dropped it by 2021; Dutch has carried its own version since 2006; Greek translated it in 2022, the year English deleted it.
- German labels the alternatives by nation. Since 2011 its Seventh says the Eighth is how "der französischen und englischen Literatur" count it, and its Edward article says English and French writing counts that one Ninth. German Wikipedia treats the modern sequence as a foreign convention, which is the project's argument about national chains stated by the editors themselves.
- French admitted a Seventh only from 2005 to 2008 (its first version said "sixième" by mistake).
- The Ninth is "sometimes grouped with the Eighth" in Spanish (2010–16), Greek (2019–21), Persian (2016–19) and Hebrew (2010–). Greek reversed English in 2022: "Ninth" stays the name and "Duke Edward's crusade" becomes the alternative. Persian kept "Ninth" as the title after the February 2020 revert but opens the article "Edward I's crusade, also known as the Ninth".

### Nine more languages

`ordinal_titles_new_languages.csv`, `navbox_new_languages.csv`.

- **Nine:** Portuguese (all nine articles and the navbox in 2005, one of the earliest complete sets anywhere), Czech (articles 2006–09), Turkish (navbox nine from 2010), Russian (Ninth article 2015).
- **Polish counts seven, and drops Frederick II.** Articles run I to VII: V is Damietta, VI and VII are Louis IX's two crusades, and there is no VIII or IX. Polish has no article on Frederick II's crusade, and its navbox omits it. This is the German seven by a different route: where German Wikipedia folds Frederick into the Fifth, Polish leaves him out.
- **Swedish and Hungarian stop early.** Swedish has articles for the First to Third; "Fjärde" to "Nionde korståget" have redirected to the main article since 2004. Hungarian stops at the Fifth; Sixth to Eighth redirect, and no Ninth title exists. Danish numbers with digits ("4. korstog") and has gaps (no 5 or 8). Norwegian has a full one-to-nine set of redirects.
- None has a Tenth.

### Wikidata

`wikidata_series.csv`. The crusade items carry series numbers only for the First to Sixth (added 2018 by the editors Odejea and Horcrux). The "follows / followed by" chain runs People's → First → … → Sixth → Barons' → Seventh and stops there; the Eighth and Ninth are outside it. The Barons' Crusade entered the chain on 18 January 2015 (Jura1), a year before English had an article. Wikidata's English label for Edward's item is still "Ninth Crusade". Small editions that build infoboxes from Wikidata therefore inherit a sequence with numbers to six, an unnumbered Barons' crusade, and no end.

### Library headings

The German National Library (`gnd_headings.csv`) uses dated headings ("Kreuzzug <1228-1229>") and keeps the numbers as variants, recording both counts: Frederick II's crusade carries "Fünfter", "5.", "Sechster" and "6." together; Louis IX's first is "Sechster", Tunis "Siebter". There is no heading for Edward's crusade. The Library of Congress could not be read: id.loc.gov answers automated access with a bot check, which I did not try to get past. LCSH needs to be checked by hand.

### Talk archives

- **English, April 2005.** Editors decided whether "Tenth Crusade" deserved coverage by comparing Google hits: 732 for "tenth crusade" against 971 for "ninth crusade".
- **German, 19 February 2007** (Redaktion Geschichte/Qualitätssicherung, rev 28204090). The discussion that produced the 2007 renames cited Mayer's *Geschichte der Kreuzzüge*, Setton, the *Lexikon des Mittelalters* and the timeline in Riley-Smith's *Illustrierte Geschichte der Kreuzzüge* (1999). One editor noted that "im anglo-amerikanischen Raum" the Fifth means Damietta; another that by Mayer's count Frederick's is the Sixth. The compromise was to name both and make "Fünfter Kreuzzug" a disambiguation page.
- **English, October 2019.** During the Good Article review of "Crusades", the text said the crusades are "traditionally counted as nine", a convention "used in 1820 by historian Charles Mills". The reviewer deleted it as possible original research on 15 October 2019, a month before the Ninth was renamed. Wikipedia had an origin story for the nine, unsourced, naming Mills. The project already has Mills in the pre-1885 American chain; whether his 1820 *History of the Crusades* has nine is now worth checking directly.
- **English, December 2021.** Dr. Grampinator, who built the List of Crusades: unable to find "a single modern work that limits itself to the numbered Crusades".

### The 2016 Barons' Crusade article

Its sources were modern surveys: Madden's *New Concise History of the Crusades* (2006), Tyerman's *God's War*, Jean Richard's *The Crusades*, and Burgtorf's encyclopedia entry on the Battle of Gaza (1239). All name the expedition rather than number it, which is why Wikipedia added it unnumbered.

## Pages that contradict themselves

The highest number a page uses and the number it states often disagree. English named a Ninth from 2013 to 2016 while its lead said "seven major Crusades." Spanish linked a Novena from 2017 to 2020 while saying there were eight. The French lead in 2022–24 said both "On compte habituellement neuf croisades" and "les sept principales croisades ultérieures" (eight in all). German states seven and its navigation box prints eight in brackets. The dataset keeps both measures, `highest_ordinal` and `stated_count`, instead of choosing between them.

## The two questions in the brief

**1. Templates against articles.** Options:

- (a) Treat the navigation box as authoritative. It is the list every crusade page displays, and the nearest thing Wikipedia has to Bouillet's dictionary entry. But some boxes are late (Dutch 2011, French 2013) and some are silent on alternatives.
- (b) Treat the main article as authoritative. It exists from 2002 and states counts in words. But its text is noisy: ordinals appear in asides, counts contradict the body, and it changes more often.
- (c) Keep both as separate observations and name the disagreement as a finding.

The data does (c): each page is its own lane, and nothing is reconciled. For a single headline number per language and year, (a) with (b) as a fallback before the box exists is the cleanest. Your call.

**2. Number in a title against number in a lead sentence.** Options:

- (a) Two levels: a number in a title or list label counts; a number in prose ("sometimes counted as the Ninth") is recorded but does not count.
- (b) Three levels: title, label, aside. Bracketed alternatives (German navigation box) and "sometimes" clauses become asides with their own status.
- (c) Record only stated counts and titles; drop asides.

This pass records titles and labels in `linked_codes_raw`, all ordinals including asides in `ordinals_in_text_raw`, explicit counts in `count_statements.csv`, and German bracketed alternatives in parentheses in the shorthand. That supports (b) without recoding. If you choose (b), `numberings.csv` needs a `status` value for asides, which fits the codebook's planned `named` and `unnumbered`.

## Method

All data come from the MediaWiki API, queried through the browser on 8 November 2026 (the workspace cannot reach Wikipedia directly). For each page the sample is the revision in force at 00:00 UTC on 1 July each year. Ordinals were found by pattern in each language, including Greek letter numerals, and links were resolved to expeditions through current titles, redirects and former titles from the move histories. German titles that changed meaning ("Fünfter Kreuzzug" before and after February 2007, "Achter Kreuzzug" before and after September 2010) are resolved by date. German rows, the English 2002 row, the Spanish 2003 row, the Arabic 2007 row (a vandalized state) and the Persian navbox 2016–17 (Ninth linked to English) are hand-coded from the text. Arabic, Persian and Hebrew ordinals were matched in their own scripts, with Persian digits normalized; a Persian false positive (barons in general, not the 1239 crusade) was removed by hand.

Every key transition was found by binary search over the revision history and then checked: the change is present at the cited revision and absent from its parent (13 of 13). Six random cells were re-extracted and matched. No sampled revision was a short-lived vandal version by a size test against its neighbors.

Limits:

- A change and its reversal between two 1 July samples will not show in the yearly grid. Only the key edits were pinned exactly.
- English history before 2002 is incomplete: it was imported in 2010 and may have gaps.
- "Highest ordinal" includes asides, so it measures what a page mentions, not what it endorses. Read it with `stated_count`.
- The English "Tenth Crusade" page linked from 2005 to 2010 was about a modern polemical use of the phrase (later retitled "(CounterPunch)" and deleted). It is excluded, but its deleted text cannot be checked without an administrator.
- The brief calls Wikipedia "the most-read crusade reference in most languages." Pageviews show scale (millions of readers a year in English) but there is no comparable figure for other references; see Readership.
